## 1. Editorial principles

### Visual understanding over exhaustive research

The product promise is: **Understand an emerging tech company in three minutes.**

Optimize for accurate mental models, not completeness. A fact earns space only when it changes the reader's understanding of the company, market position, customer, economics, momentum, or risk.

### One section, one lesson

Each section must answer one major question:

| Section | One lesson |
|---|---|
| Hero | What is it? |
| Where It Fits | Where is it in the industry? |
| Customer & Money | Who pays, and how? |
| Why People Choose It | Why does it win consideration? |
| Outside Perspective | What does the market say? |
| Remember This | What should stay in memory? |

Avoid dashboards filled with unrelated metrics. Use prose only when a diagram, label, or short card cannot teach the idea more clearly.

### Explain the layer

Prefer a precise category such as `AI Coding IDE`, `Inference Platform`, `Vector Database`, `Robotics Foundation Model`, or `Synthetic Data Infrastructure` over a broad label such as `AI company`.

When the company spans layers, identify its center of gravity and show adjacent expansion as secondary.

## 2. Evidence hierarchy

Use the strongest available source for each claim:

1. Official product documentation, pricing, changelog, filings, or terms.
2. Official company announcements and public executive statements.
3. Reputable reporting or direct investor announcements.
4. Named customer testimony.
5. GitHub repositories, issues, discussions, and release history.
6. Hacker News, Reddit, product forums, and other individual community accounts.

Do not flatten these into equivalent evidence. Label named customer testimony as hosted by the company when it appears only on the company's site. Label community anecdotes as individual reports.

For performance numbers, do not repeat a company benchmark as an independent fact. Write `the company claims` unless a credible external benchmark verifies it.

## 3. Research checklist

Gather only what is relevant:

- One-sentence product explanation.
- Precise industry category and layer.
- Primary audience and secondary buyer/user distinctions.
- Core product surfaces: API, application, marketplace, infrastructure, services, or hardware.
- Business model and billing unit.
- Latest officially confirmed company stage.
- Founding date or headquarters only when useful.
- Distribution or ecosystem strategy.
- Named customers or partners only when they clarify use cases or credibility.
- Main reasons to choose the product.
- Credible alternatives or adjacent companies.
- Third-party praise and recurring concerns.
- Verified official profile and documentation links.

Possible omission is a valid result. Do not manufacture symmetry between praise and concerns or between company fields.

## 4. Adaptive industry map

Choose the map based on the explanation problem.

| Company pattern | Preferred visual | Use when |
|---|---|---|
| Infrastructure or platform | Stack map | The key insight is the layer between upstream supply and downstream products. |
| Developer tool or product category | Positioning map | The reader needs to see differences along one or two meaningful axes. |
| Workflow application | Workflow map | The product's value comes from replacing or compressing steps. |
| Open-source project or protocol | Ecosystem map | Contributors, extensions, models, nodes, or integrations define the product. |
| Market with clear peer groups | Landscape map | Categories and competitors matter more than sequence. |
| Two-sided or multi-sided platform | Exchange map | Suppliers, the platform, developers, and end users form a loop. |

### Map rules

- Highlight the company immediately.
- Label upstream and downstream relationships in plain language.
- Use peers to establish category, not to create an arbitrary ranking.
- Add a disclaimer when nodes show market layers rather than verified commercial relationships.
- Do not put a tiny startup on a grand landscape merely to surround it with famous logos.
- If no diagram improves understanding, use a compact explanatory card instead.

### Positioning axes

Use only defensible axes such as:

- infrastructure-managed ↔ user-managed;
- general-purpose ↔ domain-specific;
- developer-first ↔ end-user-first;
- code-first ↔ no-code;
- model provider ↔ model aggregator;
- open ecosystem ↔ closed stack.

Avoid vague axes like `better`, `innovative`, or `AI-native` unless operationally defined.

## 5. Section recipes

### Hero

Include:

- company name;
- precise category, preferably with natural English industry language;
- explicit primary audience;
- one-sentence plain-language explanation;
- five to seven evidence-backed industry tags;
- compact business model and company stage labels when supported.

The hero must not become a metrics dashboard.

### Customer & Money

Distinguish:

- user from economic buyer;
- self-serve from enterprise;
- usage-based from subscription, marketplace take rate, services, hardware sales, or licensing;
- pricing mechanism from an exact price that may become stale.

Prefer `per output / compute-second / enterprise volume contract` to a price list unless exact rates are central to understanding.

### Why People Choose It

Use three or four reasons. Each reason needs one of:

- independently reported user experience;
- objective product capability supported by docs;
- named customer evidence;
- transparent first-party claim clearly labeled.

Good reasons explain choice: lower integration work, faster inference, model breadth, ecosystem compatibility, distribution, proprietary data, regulatory fit, or workflow compression.

Avoid generic claims such as `innovative`, `powerful`, or `easy to use` without specifics.

### Outside Perspective

Balance is not equal card counts. Surface the strongest traceable signals.

For each item:

- paraphrase rather than overquote;
- link to the exact discussion or review;
- state whether it is a single anecdote or repeated pattern;
- avoid presenting unresolved accusations as fact;
- note when a customer quote is selected by the company.

### Remember This

Write exactly three conclusions:

1. Category or layer.
2. Audience or customer.
3. Differentiation, strategic bet, or key risk.

Each conclusion should be understandable without the rest of the page.

## 6. Third-party perspective rules

Strong praise signals:

- specific latency or workflow comparison;
- production-use account with context;
- repeated praise for API consistency, model access, developer experience, or support;
- named customer explaining concrete impact.

Strong concern signals:

- pricing unpredictability or total-cost comparison;
- reliability, cold-start, rate-limit, or support problems;
- lock-in or portability friction;
- licensing, privacy, safety, or enterprise-governance gaps;
- confusing direct UI when the product is designed primarily as infrastructure.

Weak signals to exclude:

- anonymous praise without usage detail;
- affiliate comparison pages;
- SEO lists that repeat vendor marketing;
- automated social comments;
- unverified viral claims;
- a lone complaint presented as a company-wide pattern.

## 7. Unsupported and unstable information

Treat these as unstable and verify them close to delivery:

- funding stage, valuation, revenue, employee count, leadership, customers, model count, pricing, performance, availability, and official links.

Use the last confirmed round as the stage. A reported fundraising discussion is not a completed stage.

When sources conflict:

1. Prefer direct and newer primary evidence.
2. Preserve the discrepancy in the evidence ledger.
3. Use the narrower supported claim.
4. Omit the fact if the discrepancy does not change understanding.

Never invent missing customer counts, market shares, growth rates, or pricing.
